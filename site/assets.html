Why this page has a second half · today's news reads as risk-off
Knowing what each asset costs is only half the job. The other half is knowing which asset wins in which weather — because no single asset wins in all of it, and the ones that feel safest are often the ones being quietly destroyed. Below: how the same assets compare against each other right now, then the five weather patterns worth memorising. Today's news reads as risk-off.
How they compare with each other right now

Shares versus government bonds

Nifty earnings yield minus the 10-year government bond yield
Today-2.2 percentage points Its normal level-1.6 percentage points

Flip the PE ratio upside down and you get the "earnings yield" — the profit the companies earn for every ₹100 you pay. Right now shares earn you about 4.4% and a government bond pays about 6.6% with no risk at all. So you are accepting 2.2 points LESS to take equity risk.

That sounds mad until you remember bonds pay you the same rupee amount forever, while company profits grow. Over the last two decades the gap has averaged about −1.6 points, so today is slightly worse than normal but not extreme. When this gap gets to −4 or worse, shares have historically gone on to disappoint; when it turns positive, shares have been a gift.

Gold versus Indian shares

Gold price divided by the Nifty 50 level
TodayGold ahead of its 20-year normal Its normal levelLong-run normal

One ratio, one question: how many units of the Nifty does an ounce of gold buy? When gold is high on this measure, fear has been paid for generously and shares are the cheaper of the two.

Gold has already done its job for this cycle — it rose while the world was frightened. Historically, buying gold after it has run hard against shares has produced poor 5-year returns; buying it when nobody wants it has been where the money was made. This is exactly why a fixed percentage, rebalanced, beats chasing.

Small companies versus large ones

Nifty Smallcap 250 PE divided by Nifty 50 PE
Today1.25x Its normal level1.10x

Small companies usually trade a little dearer than large ones because they can grow faster. When that premium stretches far above its normal level, you are paying up twice — for growth, and for the excitement.

Every Indian small-cap mania has ended the same way: the premium peaks, then small caps fall roughly twice as hard as the Nifty and take years longer to recover. The premium, not the price level, is the warning light.

Property versus lending

Rental yield on a flat versus the 10-year government bond
Today2.8% versus 6.6% Its normal level3.4% versus 7.3%

A flat rented out returns about 2.8% a year in rent before repairs, tax and empty months. Lending the same money to the Government of India pays about 6.6% with no tenant, no broker and no maintenance.

That gap means Indian property prices already have years of future rent increases built into them. Property can still win — through leverage and forced saving — but the rent alone is no longer paying you to own it.

Cash after inflation

Bank fixed deposit rate minus tax minus inflation
TodayAbout +0.4% a year Its normal levelAbout 0.0% a year

A 7% fixed deposit at the 30% slab leaves you 4.9%. Take away inflation of about 4.5% and you have kept roughly 0.4% of buying power.

This is the real cost of "safety": over 30 years, cash roughly preserves what you have and grows almost nothing. That is fine for money you need within three years and quietly ruinous for money you need in thirty.

The five weather patterns, and what wins in each

Inflation runs hot

How you would know: CPI above 6%, food and fuel leading, RBI turning hawkish
Tends to win
  • Gold — the classic hedge, because it cannot be printed
  • Commodity producers — metals and energy sell the very things getting dearer
  • Banks, early on — they reprice loans upward faster than deposits
Tends to lose
  • Long-dated bonds — a fixed 6.6% forever is worth less as prices rise
  • Expensive growth shares — future profits discounted harder hurt them most
  • Cash and FDs — the real return goes negative
Why it works that way

Inflation is the tax that nobody votes for. It punishes anything promising you a FIXED number of rupees later, and rewards anything whose price rises with the general price level.

The India twistIndia's inflation is still mostly a food and fuel story. A bad monsoon or a crude spike does more to your portfolio than any Fed meeting, because it forces the RBI to keep rates high and squeezes the consumer companies that dominate the index.

Interest rates come down

How you would know: RBI cutting, bond yields falling, liquidity comfortable
Tends to win
  • Long-dated bonds — the price of an existing bond rises as new ones pay less
  • Small and mid caps — cheaper borrowing helps smaller, more indebted firms most
  • Property and real-estate shares — EMIs fall, buyers return
  • Gold — it competes with interest-bearing assets, so lower rates help it
Tends to lose
  • Banks, late in the cycle — loan rates fall faster than deposit costs
  • Savers rolling over FDs at lower and lower rates
Why it works that way

A falling interest rate raises the value of every future rupee. Assets whose payoff is far in the future — growth shares, long bonds, property — gain the most, which is why rate-cut cycles feel like everything works at once.

The India twistIn India, rate cuts usually arrive when inflation is beaten and growth is soft. The market front-runs them by six months, so the money is made while the news still sounds bad.

The rupee weakens fast

How you would know: FII outflows, dollar index strong, RBI selling reserves
Tends to win
  • IT services and pharma — they earn in dollars and spend in rupees
  • Gold — priced in dollars globally, so it rises in rupee terms automatically
  • International funds — your dollar assets are worth more rupees
Tends to lose
  • Oil refiners, airlines, chemical importers — their input bills are in dollars
  • Anyone holding only rupee assets — quietly poorer in world terms
Why it works that way

A currency is a price like any other. When the rupee falls, every rupee-earning asset you own is marked down against the world, and every dollar-earning asset is marked up. Most Indian portfolios are 100% on the losing side of that trade.

The India twistThe rupee has slid roughly 3% a year against the dollar for three decades. Treat that as the baseline weather, not an emergency — and own some dollar earnings so you are not betting everything on one currency.

The world gets frightened

How you would know: War, a banking scare, a tariff shock; VIX spiking, FIIs selling everything
Tends to win
  • Gold — the one asset people buy precisely because they trust nothing else
  • Government bonds — money runs to whatever cannot default
  • Cash — optionality, the ability to buy what others must sell
Tends to lose
  • Small caps — the first thing sold, because they are hardest to sell later
  • Anything bought on borrowed money
  • Emerging markets broadly, India included, regardless of local news
Why it works that way

In a panic, correlations go to one: things that normally move separately all fall together, because the seller is not choosing on merit — they simply need cash. Only genuinely different assets protect you, and there are very few.

The India twistIndia is fundamentally a domestic economy but a foreign-funded market. That is why a Nifty fall can have nothing to do with India: the seller is an overseas fund meeting redemptions in New York.

Growth is strong and boring

How you would know: Profits rising, inflation behaved, credit growing, no crisis in the news
Tends to win
  • Shares in general, and small and mid caps most of all
  • Banks — loan growth with low defaults is their sweet spot
  • Property and cyclicals — industry, autos, construction
Tends to lose
  • Gold — nobody needs insurance when nothing is burning
  • Cash — the opportunity cost of sitting out compounds
Why it works that way

This is the regime that pays owners of businesses, and it lasts longer than anyone expects. The mistake is not being absent — it is arriving late, after valuations have already priced in the good weather continuing forever.

The India twistIndia has had the longest stretches of this of any large market. It is also why Indian investors systematically underestimate how bad the other four regimes feel: most portfolios here have never been properly tested.
