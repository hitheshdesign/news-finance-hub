April 2020 · India

When a 'safe' debt fund locked the doors

Franklin Templeton abruptly shut six debt schemes holding about ₹26,000 crore. Roughly 300,000 investors could not withdraw their money, and it took more than two years to return most of it.

  1. Why it happened

    The schemes had reached for yield by holding lower-rated corporate bonds — paper that is easy to buy and extremely hard to sell in a panic. When Covid hit and investors rushed for the exit, the funds faced selling into a market with no buyers. The only alternative to a fire-sale was to stop redemptions.

  2. What it hit

    Confidence collapsed across the whole debt-fund category, not just Franklin's. A great many Indian investors learned for the first time that a debt fund is not a fixed deposit, and that it can both fall and freeze.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    SEBI imposed mandatory liquidity buffers on open-ended debt schemes, tightened concentration limits, required stress testing, and introduced swing pricing so that investors who rush out bear more of the cost of their own exit.

  4. And what that policy led to

    Debt funds became more boring and more honest. Money moved decisively toward target-maturity funds and government-backed paper, which is why those are now the sensible default for Indian debt investing.

The pattern to rememberLiquidity is not a property of an asset; it is a property of the market on the day you need to sell. Anything promising both a high yield and instant access is making a promise about other people's future behaviour.
