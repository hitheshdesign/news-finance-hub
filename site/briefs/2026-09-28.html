Pattern from history
November 2016 · India

Most of a country's cash cancelled overnight

At 8pm on 8 November, ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes — about 86% of India's currency by value — ceased to be legal tender at midnight. Queues formed outside banks for weeks.

  1. Why it happened

    The stated aims were to destroy unaccounted wealth, counterfeit notes and terror financing. The underlying logic was that illicit cash hoards would simply never be deposited, and would therefore be extinguished.

  2. What it hit

    Nearly 99% of the cancelled notes came back into the banking system, so the black-money aim largely failed. But the cash-dependent informal economy — small traders, daily-wage labour, farm markets — was badly disrupted, and growth slowed for several quarters.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    What followed mattered far more than the disruption itself. Digital payments were pushed hard: UPI, launched months earlier, was given enormous impetus, and bank account penetration jumped.

  4. And what that policy led to

    India now runs the largest real-time payments system in the world by volume. Household savings shifted out of cash and gold and into bank deposits and mutual funds — the beginning of the monthly SIP flows that now support the Indian equity market. Almost nobody predicted that from the announcement.

The pattern to rememberA policy's stated aim and its lasting effect are often different things, and the lasting effect usually comes from the infrastructure built around it. Judge policies by what they permanently changed, not by whether they hit their stated target.
