Pattern from history
July 1997 to 1998 · Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia

The crisis that taught Asia to hoard dollars

Thailand ran out of reserves defending its currency peg and let the baht float. It fell 50% in months. The panic spread through Indonesia, South Korea and Malaysia within weeks, becoming the worst emerging market crisis of the era.

  1. Why it happened

    These economies had borrowed heavily in dollars while earning in local currency — a mismatch that is invisible while the exchange rate is fixed and fatal the moment it moves. Their central banks had promised stable pegs, which encouraged still more dollar borrowing. When growth slowed and the dollar strengthened, speculators tested the pegs and the reserves ran out.

  2. What it hit

    Indonesia's economy shrank 13% in a year and its government fell. South Korea, a developed economy, needed the largest IMF bailout in history to that point. Millions fell back into poverty. Asian shares lost 60-80% in dollar terms.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    IMF bailouts with severe conditions, which remain bitterly resented in the region. More durably, the affected countries abandoned rigid pegs, let their currencies float, and began accumulating enormous foreign exchange reserves as self-insurance so they would never have to ask again.

  4. And what that policy led to

    Asia's reserve hoard is the direct descendant of 1997. Those reserves were recycled into US government bonds, which helped hold global interest rates down for two decades — one of the quiet forces behind the cheap money of the 2000s, and therefore behind the housing bubble that followed.

The pattern to rememberBorrowing in a currency you do not earn is the most reliable way to turn a slowdown into a collapse. It is invisible in good times and decisive in bad ones. Check what currency a country, a company or a project owes in.
The India echoIndia was barely touched, because the capital account was still largely closed and the rupee was not freely convertible. The lesson Indian policymakers drew — open up slowly, keep reserves large — still shapes RBI policy today, and explains why India has weathered every emerging market scare since with discomfort rather than disaster.
The modern tellCountries with large dollar debts, thin reserves and a currency the central bank insists is stable. The combination is the setup, every time.
