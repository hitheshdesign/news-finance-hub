Pattern from history
May to September 2013 · United States, felt hardest in India

A speech that cost India 20% of its currency

Fed chairman Ben Bernanke suggested in testimony that the Fed might slow its bond buying "in the next few meetings". He did not change any policy. Within four months the rupee had fallen from 55 to 68 per dollar and India was labelled one of the "Fragile Five".

  1. Why it happened

    Years of near-zero US rates had pushed investors into emerging markets hunting for yield. India ran a large current account deficit — importing far more than it exported, with gold and oil the biggest items — and was therefore dependent on that foreign money continuing to arrive. The hint that the tide might turn was enough; no actual policy change was needed.

  2. What it hit

    The rupee fell about 20% in four months. Indian bond yields spiked, the stock market fell sharply, and the RBI raised rates into an already slowing economy to defend the currency.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    The RBI opened a special concessional window to attract dollar deposits from Indians abroad, raising about $34 billion. Gold import duties were raised steeply to curb the import bill. And in 2016 India adopted a formal inflation-targeting framework, which addressed the underlying cause.

  4. And what that policy led to

    India's current account deficit narrowed sharply and reserves were rebuilt to levels that made the 2018 and 2022 dollar shocks far less painful. The gold import duty also created a large smuggling industry — an unintended consequence that took years to unwind.

The pattern to rememberAn economy dependent on foreign capital is hostage to a foreign central bank's tone, not just its decisions. The vulnerability is built during the good years, when the money is arriving and nobody asks why.
