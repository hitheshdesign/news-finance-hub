Pattern from history
October 1973 · Middle East, felt globally

The embargo that rewired the world economy

Arab oil producers cut output and embargoed several countries during the Yom Kippur war. Oil went from about $3 a barrel to $12 within months, and the industrial world discovered how completely it depended on a resource it did not control.

  1. Why it happened

    Oil demand had grown for two decades on the assumption of permanently cheap supply, so there was no spare capacity and no stockpile anywhere. The producers realised they had collective pricing power and used it. A political event became an economic one because no buffer existed.

  2. What it hit

    Stagflation — high inflation and high unemployment together, which economists had thought nearly impossible. US shares fell about 48%. Petrol rationing across the West. For oil-importing India, the import bill and inflation both surged.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    Strategic petroleum reserves were created so a political shock could not immediately become a price shock. Fuel-efficiency standards were imposed on cars. Nuclear and, much later, renewable programmes were accelerated. The International Energy Agency was founded to coordinate responses.

  4. And what that policy led to

    Rich-world oil consumption per unit of output has more than halved since. The reserves and efficiency rules genuinely worked, which is why later oil shocks — including 2022 — hurt considerably less than 1973 did.

The pattern to rememberA concentrated supply of something essential is a standing political risk, whatever the price chart says. The response is always the same — build a buffer, use less, find alternatives — and it takes about a decade to work.
