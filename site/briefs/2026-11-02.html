Pattern from history
June to August 2015 · China

A state-encouraged bubble and a surprise devaluation

Chinese shares doubled in a year, partly on state media encouragement, then fell about 40% in three months. In August the central bank unexpectedly devalued the yuan, and world markets fell sharply over two days.

  1. Why it happened

    Retail investors, many with newly opened accounts and little experience, had bought heavily on margin loans. When prices turned, margin calls forced selling, which forced more margin calls. The devaluation then signalled that China's slowdown was worse than official figures admitted, which hit everyone selling into Chinese demand.

  2. What it hit

    Commodities collapsed — copper, iron ore and oil all fell hard, since China consumed roughly half the world's industrial metals. Emerging market currencies including the rupee weakened. Indian metal shares fell sharply despite nothing changing in India.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    Trading halts, a ban on large shareholders selling, state funds instructed to buy, and tighter capital controls to stop money leaving. Later, a managed and better-communicated currency mechanism.

  4. And what that policy led to

    It exposed the limits of administrative intervention in a market: the measures slowed the fall but damaged foreign investors' trust in Chinese market access for years. That trust deficit is part of why global money has been reallocating toward India since.

The pattern to rememberRetail leverage is the accelerant. A market where ordinary investors are buying with borrowed money does not correct gently, because the selling is involuntary. Margin data is one of the few genuinely predictive warnings.
