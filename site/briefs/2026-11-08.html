Pattern from history
April 1992 · India

The scam that built India's market plumbing

A journalist revealed that broker Harshad Mehta had been using forged bank receipts to divert money from the interbank government bond market into shares, driving the Sensex up over 140% in a year. When the mechanism was exposed the index fell roughly 40%.

  1. Why it happened

    The bond settlement system relied on paper receipts and personal trust between banks. There was no electronic record, no central depository, and no regulator with real teeth. Shares settled fortnightly, which let a broker build enormous positions with no money down. The fraud was not clever so much as inevitable given the plumbing.

  2. What it hit

    About ₹4,000 crore of public bank money was diverted — enormous for the time. Several bank chairmen were arrested. Retail investors who had bought into the boom lost heavily, and Indian equity took years to recover its reputation with ordinary savers.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    SEBI, which had existed only as an advisory body, was given statutory powers in 1992. The National Stock Exchange was created as an electronic, screen-based competitor to the broker-controlled BSE. Shares were dematerialised into depositories, and physical certificates disappeared.

  4. And what that policy led to

    This is why buying a share in India today is instant, electronic and safe, settled by a central depository with a regulator behind it — infrastructure that many richer countries took longer to build. The scandal's cost bought the market's plumbing.

The pattern to rememberFinancial scandals almost never come from exotic instruments. They come from a settlement gap, a trust-based process, or a regulator without authority. Ask where the paperwork is manual and who checks it.
