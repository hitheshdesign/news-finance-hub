Pattern from history
March 2023 · United States

A bank run at the speed of a smartphone

Silicon Valley Bank lost $42 billion of deposits in a single day and was shut by regulators within about 48 hours of the first sign of trouble — the fastest bank run in history.

  1. Why it happened

    The bank had taken enormous deposits during the technology boom and put them into long-dated government bonds, which carry no credit risk at all. When rates rose sharply in 2022, those bonds lost market value. The holdings were fine if held to maturity and not if it had to sell — and over 90% of its deposits were above the insured limit, so depositors had every reason to run first and ask later. Word spread through group chats.

  2. What it hit

    Two more US banks failed within days, and Credit Suisse — unrelated but fragile — was forced into a shotgun merger. Bank shares fell worldwide. Gold rose, as it does whenever people start questioning banks.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    US regulators guaranteed all deposits at the failed banks, insured or not, and opened a facility letting banks borrow against bonds at face value rather than market value. Supervision of mid-sized banks was tightened.

  4. And what that policy led to

    The rule that only insured deposits are protected was effectively suspended in practice, which stores up its own future problem. It also made interest-rate risk, rather than credit risk, the first thing bank supervisors now ask about.

The pattern to rememberA government bond has no default risk and plenty of price risk, and that distinction stays invisible until somebody is forced to sell. Meanwhile concentrated, uninsured depositors are the modern queue outside the door — and they move in minutes now, not days.
