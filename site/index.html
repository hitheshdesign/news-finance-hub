Pattern from history
August 1971 · United States

The day the dollar stopped being gold

President Nixon went on television on a Sunday evening and announced that the United States would no longer convert dollars into gold for foreign governments. The system that had governed world money since 1944 ended in a fifteen-minute broadcast, and it was never restored.

  1. Why it happened

    Under the Bretton Woods agreement every currency was pegged to the dollar and the dollar was pegged to gold at $35 an ounce. That worked only while America held enough gold to honour the promise. Spending on the Vietnam war and on domestic programmes meant far more dollars circulating abroad than America had gold to back. France and Britain began asking for the gold. The promise was arithmetically impossible to keep, and everyone could see it.

  2. What it hit

    Currencies began floating against each other for the first time in modern history, which is why exchange rates move every day now. The dollar fell about a third against major currencies over two years. Gold, freed from its fixed price, went from $35 to $850 by 1980. Inflation across the rich world moved into double digits, because the external discipline that had capped money creation was simply gone.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    The immediate response was wage and price controls — which failed within two years. The durable answer took another decade: central banks were made independent of elected governments and given an explicit inflation target, so that the discipline gold had imposed from outside was rebuilt inside the institutions instead.

  4. And what that policy led to

    We live in that world now. Money is a promise backed by an institution's credibility rather than by metal, which is precisely why central bank independence is defended so fiercely and why any political interference with a central bank frightens markets so much. It also made currencies a permanent variable in every cross-border investment — the reason a rupee investor has to think about the dollar at all.

The pattern to rememberWhen a fixed promise becomes arithmetically impossible, it does not get renegotiated gradually. It gets abandoned suddenly, usually on a weekend, and the people who prepared for the possibility do far better than the ones who trusted the promise because it had always held.
The India echoIndia's own version came twenty years later, in 1991, for the same underlying reason: a promise the reserves could not fund. Today the echo is quieter but constant — the rupee's long slide against the dollar is the background weather of every Indian portfolio, and it exists because 1971 made currencies float.
The modern tellAny peg or price cap that requires ever-larger reserves to defend. Currency pegs, fuel price freezes, and support levels that "will always hold" all end the same way.
