Pattern from history
2007 to 2009 · United States, then everywhere

When the safest thing in finance turned out not to be

American house prices fell, mortgage securities that were rated as safe as government bonds proved worthless, and Lehman Brothers was allowed to fail. Global credit markets stopped functioning within days.

  1. Why it happened

    Mortgages were bundled into securities, sliced into tranches, and rated AAA on the assumption that house prices would never fall across the whole country at once. That assumption had held for decades, so it was treated as a law. Banks held these with very little of their own capital behind them, and insured each other through derivatives — which concentrated the risk rather than spreading it.

  2. What it hit

    Global shares fell more than 50%. World trade collapsed faster than in 1929. Unemployment doubled in the US and Europe. India's Sensex fell about 60% and the rupee slid sharply, despite Indian banks holding almost none of the toxic assets — a pure demonstration that a foreign-funded market imports foreign panic.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    Bank bailouts, then zero interest rates and quantitative easing — central banks creating money to buy bonds — on a scale never attempted before. Regulation followed: Basel III forced banks to hold far more capital, and Dodd-Frank restricted what they could trade with their own money.

  4. And what that policy led to

    Banks became safer and far less profitable, and much risky lending simply migrated to less-regulated non-bank lenders — where it still sits. A decade of near-zero rates inflated the price of every asset and widened wealth inequality sharply, which reshaped the politics of the following decade.

The pattern to rememberThe dangerous asset is never the one everybody calls risky. It is the one everybody calls safe, because that is where leverage accumulates unexamined. Ask what today's "obviously safe" holding is, and what would have to be true for it not to be.
