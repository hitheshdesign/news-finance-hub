Pattern from history
June to July 1991 · India

India pledged its gold and changed course

With foreign reserves down to about two weeks of imports, India physically flew 47 tonnes of gold to the Bank of England as collateral for an emergency loan. Within weeks the government devalued the rupee twice and began dismantling the licensing system that had governed Indian industry since independence.

  1. Why it happened

    Decades of import substitution had produced an economy that could not earn enough foreign exchange. The Gulf war spiked oil prices and cut remittances from Indians working in the Gulf simultaneously. The Soviet Union, a major trading partner, was collapsing. Political instability meant credit lines were pulled. Several slow problems arrived together with one fast shock — the usual anatomy of a crisis.

  2. What it hit

    The rupee fell about 20% in two days. Inflation hit 13%. But what followed was the most consequential economic change in modern Indian history: industrial licensing was abolished, import tariffs that reached 300% began falling, and foreign investment was allowed in.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    Liberalisation — of licensing, trade and investment — plus a managed floating rupee instead of a fixed one, and a deliberate policy of accumulating foreign exchange reserves so it could never happen again.

  4. And what that policy led to

    India's growth rate roughly doubled over the following two decades. The companies that dominate the Nifty today — the private banks, the IT exporters, the consumer giants — are largely creatures of the post-1991 settlement. And the reserve-hoarding instinct stuck: India now holds well over a year of import cover, which is why rupee wobbles since have been uncomfortable rather than existential.

The pattern to rememberCrises are when structural reform becomes politically possible. Changes that were unthinkable for forty years were passed in weeks because the alternative was default. The investment lesson is the flip side: the point of maximum fear is often the start of the best decade.
