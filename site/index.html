Pattern from history
September 2018 · India

The AAA company that could not pay

IL&FS, an infrastructure lender rated AAA weeks earlier, missed payments on its short-term borrowings. India's entire non-bank lending sector froze within days, and debt mutual funds holding its paper had to mark down investors' money.

  1. Why it happened

    It borrowed short-term at low rates to fund infrastructure that would pay back over decades — a maturity mismatch that works perfectly until the day nobody rolls over your short-term borrowing. Its group ran to roughly 350 subsidiaries, which made the true debt load nearly impossible to see from outside. And the rating agencies that called it AAA were paid by it.

  2. What it hit

    Non-bank lenders' funding costs jumped. Housing finance companies stopped lending, which hit developers, which hit construction jobs. This is a large part of why Indian growth slowed through 2019, well before Covid. Several debt funds gated withdrawals.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    The government superseded the board. The RBI brought large non-bank lenders under far closer supervision, imposed liquidity coverage requirements on them, and limited how much short-term money they could rely on. SEBI tightened what debt funds were allowed to hold.

  4. And what that policy led to

    Indian non-bank lending is meaningfully safer and slower than it was, and credit-risk funds never recovered their popularity. It also drove consolidation: weak lenders were absorbed and the survivors, mostly bank-backed, took the market.

The pattern to rememberMaturity mismatch is the oldest failure in finance, and a credit rating is an opinion bought by the borrower. When an institution borrows short to lend long, the question is not whether it is profitable but who refinances it on a bad day.
