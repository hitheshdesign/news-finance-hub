Pattern from history
1989 to 2024 · Japan

The bubble that took thirty years to repay

At its 1989 peak the Tokyo stock market was worth more than the American one, and the land under the Imperial Palace was said to be worth more than all of California. The Nikkei then fell about 80% and did not regain its 1989 high until 2024 — thirty-five years later.

  1. Why it happened

    The 1985 Plaza Accord pushed the yen sharply higher, hurting exporters. The Bank of Japan cut rates hard to compensate, and the cheap money went into land and shares rather than into business investment. Banks lent against property using property as collateral, so rising prices justified more lending, which raised prices further. A closed loop, and everyone inside it could point to a rational-sounding reason.

  2. What it hit

    Households and banks spent two decades repairing balance sheets instead of spending and lending. Growth flatlined, prices actually fell for years, and a generation entered a frozen job market. Japan's share of world output fell from roughly 15% to under 5%.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    Rates went to zero and later below it. Japan invented quantitative easing in 2001 — a central bank creating money to buy government bonds — because conventional rate cuts had run out of room. Banks were eventually forced to recognise bad loans instead of rolling them over.

  4. And what that policy led to

    Every major central bank used Japan's playbook in 2008 and again in 2020, which is why zero rates and bond-buying stopped being exotic. Japan also proved an economy can stagnate for decades without a dramatic collapse — the danger is not always a crash.

The pattern to rememberWhen an asset is bought with debt secured against that same asset, the cycle feeds itself upward and then downward with equal force. Ask what the collateral is; if the collateral is the thing being bought, you are looking at the mechanism.
The India echoIndian property lending and promoter share-pledging are the same shape on a smaller scale. It is why the RBI watches loan-to-value ratios, and why a promoter pledging shares to buy more shares deserves real suspicion.
The modern tellLending growth running far ahead of income growth, with the asset itself as the security. That combination has never ended gently.
