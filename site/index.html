When a real revolution was still a terrible investment

The Nasdaq fell 78% over two and a half years. Companies that had listed with no profits and enormous valuations went to zero in their hundreds. The internet, meanwhile, continued to transform the world exactly as promised.

  1. Why it happened

    The story was true and the price was wrong — which is the hardest lesson in investing. Capital flooded in because the technology was genuinely revolutionary, so valuations stopped referring to earnings at all and began referring to page views and "eyeballs". Cheap money after the 1998 crisis supplied the fuel; investment bank research supplied the justification.

  2. What it hit

    About $5 trillion of market value vanished. It took the Nasdaq fifteen years to regain its 2000 peak. Yet Amazon, which fell 94%, went on to become one of the most valuable companies on earth. Both facts are true at once.

  3. The policy brought in to fix it

    Sarbanes-Oxley in 2002 forced chief executives to personally certify their accounts and made auditors independent of consulting arms. Rules were introduced to separate investment bank research from the deal-making desks that had been quietly paying for it.

  4. And what that policy led to

    Corporate accounts became more trustworthy and the cost of going public rose sharply — which is part of why companies now stay private for far longer and list only when they are already large. The Fed also cut rates hard to cushion the bust, which fed the housing boom that broke in 2008.

The pattern to rememberA transformative technology and a good investment are different questions. The revolution can be entirely real while the shares are still priced for a future that arrives ten years late. Ask what you are paying for the growth, not whether the growth is coming.
The India echoIndia had its own version. The Sensex IT index collapsed with the Nasdaq and Indian software stocks lost 80%. But Infosys and TCS were real businesses with real dollar earnings, and they compounded for the next twenty years. Today's Indian AI and new-age tech listings are the same question in new clothes.
